**Introduction**

Model risk has emerged as a critical issue in financial modeling and valuation practices, particularly concerning its implications within the context of Value-at-Risk (VaR) models used to assess market risks associated with trading portfolios. This study aims to delve into the intricacies of how VaR models, when employed with historical simulation techniques, can inadvertently introduce significant distortions or biases that may lead to suboptimal risk management decisions. By examining these issues through a historical-simulation lens, this research seeks to provide insights into the potential pitfalls associated with relying on such methodologies and suggests strategies for mitigating these effects.

The focus on the trading book aspect of traditional banking operations underscores the importance of accurate risk assessment frameworks in maintaining stability and profitability within high-stakes investment environments. Traditional approaches to measuring risk often rely heavily on quantitative analysis methods like VaR, which have historically been pivotal tools for regulatory compliance and internal stress testing purposes. However, the application of these models, especially under scenarios involving non-normal distributions and extreme events, raises several questions regarding their reliability and applicability.

Historical simulation-based VaR models, which rely on past data to estimate future risk, face particular challenges when applied to real-time markets characterized by frequent volatility shifts, sudden changes in economic conditions, and the unpredictable nature of asset prices. These factors not only complicate the estimation process but also introduce elements of randomness and uncertainty into the VaR calculations, potentially leading to underestimation or overestimation of true risk profiles.

In essence, the purpose of this paper is twofold: first, to critically analyze the theoretical foundations underlying historical simulation in VaR models; secondly, to explore empirical evidence from case studies and industry practices that illustrate instances where historical simulation has failed to adequately capture the inherent complexity and variability of trading book performance. Through this examination, we seek to offer a deeper understanding of the limitations and vulnerabilities inherent in using historical simulation techniques, thereby informing practitioners, regulators, and academics about best practices and future directions for improving risk measurement methodologies in financial institutions.

By addressing these complexities head-on, our paper endeavors to contribute to ongoing debates around the development of robust, forward-looking risk management systems that can effectively handle both normal and extraordinary market conditions. Such advancements are essential for ensuring the integrity of financial markets and enhancing the resilience of financial institutions against systemic risks.
