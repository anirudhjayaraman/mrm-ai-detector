**Supervisory Guidance on the Expected Credit Loss Model under IFRS 9: Macroeconomic Scenario Considerations**

**Introduction**
The International Financial Reporting Standards (IFRS) 9 framework requires banks to estimate credit losses over the contractual life of a loan or receivable, incorporating changes in economic conditions using the expected credit loss ("ECL") model. This regulatory requirement underscores the importance of accurate stress testing to manage risk effectively.

**Expected Credit Loss Model Implementation**
Under IFRS 9, the ECL model should be applied at initial recognition and subsequently updated based on observable data, internal models, or third-party data sources. The bank must also evaluate whether its current assumptions meet the requirements for measuring ECL under both the "current period" and "forward-looking" approaches.

**Macroeconomic Scenarios and Stress Testing Requirements**
For comprehensive stress testing purposes, the bank is expected to consider multiple macroeconomic scenarios that capture various potential adverse developments affecting borrowers' financial performance, creditworthiness, or economic outlook. These scenarios should cover diverse sectors and regions as comprehensively as possible. 

**Quantitative Results and Thresholds**
To ensure robustness, the bank's stress tests should incorporate test statistics that are statistically significant (e.g., p-values less than 5%) and adhere to predefined threshold levels. For instance, if considering five different economic scenarios, the bank might apply a sensitivity analysis where one scenario has a 10% probability impact on the net interest income. If the ECL estimates across all scenarios remain below 2% of total loans and advances (assuming an appropriate buffer), it would generally be acceptable unless there are compelling reasons to adjust these thresholds.

**Sample Size and Date Requirements**
The bank’s stress test samples should be representative and sufficient to reflect real-world variability. For instance, historical data spanning 5-7 years could provide robust insights into future risks. The date coverage should align with the frequency of the bank’s reporting cycles to maintain consistency between management’s forecasts and reported figures.

**Conclusion**
Stress testing under IFRS 9 is a critical component for ensuring accurate measurement of ECL and adequate provision for expected credit losses. Banks must meticulously design their stress scenarios to accurately depict economic conditions while adhering to statistical standards and practical operational constraints. Regular review and validation of the methodologies employed are essential to maintain high-quality stress testing practices.
