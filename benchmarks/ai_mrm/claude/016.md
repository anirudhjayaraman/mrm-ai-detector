# Introduction

Financial institutions rely on quantitative models for an ever-widening range of activities, from pricing derivatives and measuring market risk to estimating credit losses, setting regulatory capital, and making automated lending decisions. This reliance brings substantial benefits in terms of consistency, scalability, and analytical rigour. It also creates a distinct source of risk: the possibility that a model is wrong, or is used in ways for which it was not designed. This risk, commonly referred to as model risk, has become a central concern for practitioners, regulators, and academics alike.

The consequences of model risk can be severe. The 2007–2009 financial crisis exposed the limitations of models that relied on historical correlations and assumed stable market conditions, most notably in the pricing of structured credit products. More recently, the 2012 "London Whale" episode demonstrated how errors in the implementation of a value-at-risk model, compounded by weak governance, could contribute to losses exceeding $6 billion. These events have prompted a significant tightening of supervisory expectations, beginning with the Federal Reserve's SR 11-7 guidance in 2011 and continuing with the Prudential Regulation Authority's SS1/23 in the United Kingdom.

Despite this attention, the quantification of model risk remains an open problem. Supervisory guidance has largely focused on governance and process—model inventories, independent validation, and effective challenge—rather than on methods for measuring the magnitude of model risk in a given application. Existing academic approaches fall broadly into two categories. The first measures model risk as the dispersion of outputs across a set of plausible alternative models, as in the worst-case approach of Cont (2006). The second treats model uncertainty through a Bayesian lens, averaging over models according to their posterior plausibility. Both approaches face practical challenges: the first requires the specification of a set of alternative models, which is itself subject to judgement, while the second can be computationally demanding and sensitive to prior choices.

In this paper, we propose a framework for quantifying model risk based on the Wasserstein distance between the distribution implied by a reference model and a neighbourhood of alternative distributions consistent with observed data. Our approach offers several advantages. It does not require the explicit enumeration of alternative models; it provides a natural link between the size of the uncertainty set and the statistical evidence available; and it yields tractable bounds for a broad class of risk measures and pricing functionals.

We make three contributions. First, we derive closed-form expressions for the worst-case value-at-risk and expected shortfall over a Wasserstein ball, and show how the radius can be calibrated using finite-sample concentration results. Second, we apply the framework to the pricing of exotic options under stochastic volatility, showing that model risk can account for a substantial share of the bid–ask spread observed in practice. Third, we illustrate how the approach can be used to set model risk reserves in a manner consistent with supervisory expectations.

The remainder of the paper is organised as follows. Section 2 reviews the related literature. Section 3 introduces the framework and derives the main theoretical results. Section 4 presents the empirical applications, and Section 5 concludes.
