## 4. Model Validation

### 4.1 General Expectations

Model validation is the set of processes and activities intended to verify that models are performing as expected, in line with their design objectives and business uses. Effective validation helps ensure that models are sound and identifies potential limitations and assumptions, as well as their possible impact. Institutions should establish a validation function that is independent of model development and use, and that has the necessary authority, resources, and expertise to provide effective challenge.

All models should be subject to validation before they are put into use, and periodically thereafter. The scope and rigour of validation should be commensurate with the institution's overall use of models, the complexity and materiality of its models, and the size and complexity of its operations. While the extent of validation may vary, the underlying principles apply to all models.

### 4.2 Core Elements

An effective validation framework should include three core elements:

(a) **Evaluation of conceptual soundness.** This involves assessing the quality of the model design and construction, including a review of documentation and empirical evidence supporting the methods used and the variables selected. Validators should assess whether the model's assumptions are reasonable and whether its limitations are understood and documented.

(b) **Ongoing monitoring.** This confirms that the model is appropriately implemented and is being used and is performing as intended. Monitoring should assess whether changes in products, exposures, activities, clients, or market conditions necessitate adjustment, redevelopment, or replacement of the model.

(c) **Outcomes analysis.** This compares model outputs to corresponding actual outcomes. The precise nature of the comparison depends on the objectives of the model and might include an assessment of the accuracy of estimates or forecasts, an evaluation of rank-ordering ability, or other appropriate tests. Back-testing is one form of outcomes analysis.

### 4.3 Independence

Validation should generally be performed by staff who are not responsible for model development or use and who do not have a stake in whether a model is determined to be valid. Where institutions rely on the same staff for development and validation, as may be the case in smaller institutions, they should ensure that sufficient compensating controls are in place, such as review by internal audit or by an external party.

### 4.4 Vendor and Third-Party Models

The use of vendor and third-party models does not diminish an institution's responsibility for managing model risk. Institutions should require vendors to provide appropriate documentation of model design, testing results, and known limitations. Where access to proprietary information is limited, institutions should place greater emphasis on outcomes analysis, sensitivity analysis, and benchmarking.

### 4.5 Findings and Remediation

Validation findings should be documented, communicated to model owners and senior management, and tracked to resolution. Institutions should establish clear policies regarding the actions to be taken when validation identifies significant weaknesses, including restrictions on model use, the application of adjustments or overlays, and timelines for remediation.
