Abstract

Banks have been slow to adopt machine learning for regulatory credit risk models, and the usual explanation is that the models are black boxes. We argue that this framing is too coarse. Using loan-level data on 1.2 million UK mortgages originated between 2005 and 2018, we compare a logistic scorecard, a gradient-boosted tree ensemble, and a monotone-constrained variant of the latter. The unconstrained ensemble improves out-of-time AUC by 3.1 points over the scorecard, but roughly two-thirds of that gain survives when we impose monotonicity on the eleven variables a supervisor would expect to behave monotonically. Shapley-value attributions from the constrained model are also markedly more stable across re-estimation windows. Our results suggest that the relevant trade-off is not accuracy versus interpretability in the abstract, but the price of specific, testable restrictions.

1. Introduction

Internal ratings-based (IRB) models determine a large share of the capital held by European and UK banks, and supervisors scrutinise them accordingly. Under the EBA guidelines and the PRA's supervisory statements, a bank must be able to show that each risk driver makes economic sense, that the model is stable over time, and that its outputs can be explained to the people who rely on them. These requirements were written with logistic regression in mind. It is not obvious how they apply to a model with several hundred trees and thousands of split points.

Much of the existing literature treats explainability as a property that post-hoc methods can bolt on. SHAP, LIME and partial dependence plots are now standard, and several papers (Bussmann et al., 2021; Bracke et al., 2019) show that they can produce plausible-looking explanations for credit models. But plausible-looking is not the same as reliable. Attributions can change substantially when a model is retrained on a slightly different sample, even when predictive performance does not, and a supervisor reviewing a model once every few years has no way of knowing which explanation to believe.

We take a different route. Rather than asking whether a complex model can be explained after the fact, we ask how much predictive power is lost when the model is constrained up front to satisfy the properties a supervisor actually checks—principally monotonicity in key risk drivers. If the loss is small, the case against machine learning in IRB is weaker than commonly supposed. If it is large, we learn something about where the extra accuracy is coming from.
