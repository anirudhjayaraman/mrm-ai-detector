## 2. Related Literature

The backtesting of risk measures has a long history in both the academic literature and regulatory practice. For value-at-risk (VaR), the problem is comparatively well understood. Kupiec (1995) proposed the unconditional coverage test based on the frequency of exceptions, while Christoffersen (1998) extended this framework to test the independence of exceptions over time, combining the two into a test of conditional coverage. These tests remain the cornerstone of regulatory backtesting under the Basel framework, which assigns models to green, yellow, or red zones based on the number of exceptions observed over a 250-day window.

The shift from VaR to expected shortfall (ES) under the Fundamental Review of the Trading Book (FRTB) has raised new challenges. Unlike VaR, ES accounts for the magnitude of losses beyond the quantile, addressing a well-known shortcoming of VaR as a measure of tail risk. However, Gneiting (2011) showed that ES is not elicitable—that is, there exists no scoring function for which ES is the unique minimiser of expected score. This result was initially interpreted as implying that ES could not be backtested, prompting considerable debate.

Subsequent work has clarified that elicitability is not a necessary condition for backtestability. Acerbi and Székely (2014) proposed three model-free tests for ES that exploit the relationship between ES and the conditional distribution of losses in the tail. Their second test, in particular, has attracted attention due to its simplicity and power. Fissler, Ziegel, and Gneiting (2016) showed that ES is jointly elicitable with VaR, enabling comparative backtests based on joint scoring functions. Nolde and Ziegel (2017) developed this idea further, distinguishing between traditional backtests, which assess whether a model is correct, and comparative backtests, which assess whether one model is better than another.

A separate strand of the literature has focused on regression-based approaches. Engle and Manganelli (2004) introduced the dynamic quantile test, which regresses exception indicators on lagged information. Building on this, Bayer and Dimitriadis (2022) proposed a regression-based ES backtest that tests whether the ES forecasts are calibrated by regressing realised returns on the forecasts within a joint VaR–ES regression framework. Spectral backtests, as proposed by Gordy and McNeil (2020), generalise coverage tests by weighting exceedances across multiple quantile levels according to a chosen kernel.

Despite these advances, important questions remain. Most existing tests have limited power in small samples, which is a particular concern given the 250-day windows typical in practice. Moreover, the performance of these tests under realistic features of financial data—including volatility clustering, heavy tails, and structural breaks—has received relatively limited attention. Our paper contributes to this literature by providing a systematic comparison of ES backtests under these conditions.
