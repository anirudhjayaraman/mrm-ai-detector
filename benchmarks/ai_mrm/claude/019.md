Re: Request for Information on Financial Institutions' Use of Artificial Intelligence, Including Machine Learning

Dear Sir or Madam,

We appreciate the opportunity to respond to the agencies' request for information on the use of artificial intelligence (AI), including machine learning (ML), by financial institutions. As a regional bank with approximately $85 billion in assets, we use ML techniques in a growing number of applications, including fraud detection, anti-money laundering monitoring, and marketing analytics. We have more limited use of ML in credit underwriting, where we continue to rely primarily on traditional scorecards.

Our comments focus on model risk management, which we believe is the area where additional supervisory clarity would be most valuable.

**Applicability of existing guidance.** We believe that the existing model risk management guidance, including SR 11-7 and OCC Bulletin 2011-12, provides a sound foundation for managing the risks of AI and ML models. The principles-based nature of the guidance allows institutions to adapt their practices to new techniques. We would not support the development of separate guidance specific to AI, which could create inconsistency and complexity.

**Explainability.** We would welcome clarification of supervisory expectations for explainability. In particular, it would be helpful for the agencies to confirm that post-hoc explanation techniques, such as SHAP values, can be relied upon to support conceptual soundness assessments, and to clarify the level of explainability expected for different use cases. The explainability needed for a fraud model, where decisions can be reviewed and reversed, differs from that required for a credit decision subject to adverse action notice requirements.

**Dynamic updating.** Some ML models are designed to be retrained frequently. Under our current framework, each retraining could constitute a model change requiring validation, which is impractical. We would encourage the agencies to clarify that institutions may validate the retraining process itself, including the controls and performance thresholds that govern it, rather than each individual retrained model.

**Third-party models.** Many institutions, particularly smaller ones, rely on vendor-provided AI solutions. Vendors are often reluctant to share proprietary details, which limits validation. We encourage the agencies to consider ways to facilitate greater transparency from vendors, such as through industry standards for model documentation.

We would be pleased to discuss these comments further.

Sincerely,

Chief Risk Officer
